Anxiety before a job interview can make a prepared person feel as if they forgot everything.

You may have read the job description, looked up the company, and thought through examples from your work. Then, ten minutes before the interview, your chest gets tight, your thoughts start racing, and every answer suddenly sounds wrong in your head.

That does not mean you need more preparation. It often means your body has shifted into a stress response. A short reset cannot promise a perfect interview or control the questions you get. It can help you slow down enough to use the preparation you already have.

## Why interviews can make your body go into overdrive

An interview asks you to be assessed by people you may not know, under a time limit, with an outcome that can matter a great deal. Your body does not need to decide whether the situation is objectively dangerous before it starts preparing for it.

That can look like shallow breathing, a dry mouth, tense shoulders, a fast heartbeat, or a mind that keeps jumping to the worst possible answer. You may start treating every pause as a problem before the interview has even begun.

The goal is not to talk yourself out of caring. It is to make enough room around the nerves that you can listen, think, and answer one question at a time. If anxiety shows up in many parts of your life, [meditation for anxiety](/blog/meditation-for-anxiety) offers a broader way to work with racing thoughts and body tension. For an interview, keep the job smaller.

## Do not spend the last five minutes rehearsing every answer

Last-minute review can be useful if it helps you remember a name, a question you want to ask, or one example you want ready. It stops helping when you begin trying to predict the whole conversation.

You cannot script every question, follow-up, or reaction. Trying to do so often makes your thoughts more crowded. You may enter the interview focused on matching an internal script instead of hearing the person in front of you.

Use the last few minutes for a short list instead:

- the role you are discussing
- two or three work examples you can draw from
- one question you genuinely want to ask
- the main point you want them to understand about how you work

Then close the notes. You have prepared enough for this moment. More rehearsal is unlikely to create a new answer worth having.

## Loosen the tension your body is carrying

Before you join a video call or walk into the building, check the places where tension tends to collect.

Let your feet rest on the floor. Unclench your jaw. Lower your shoulders a little. If you have been holding your phone or résumé tightly, put it down for a moment and let your hands rest.

This is not a performance trick. It is a simple way to notice that your body has been bracing. A few small adjustments can make it easier to breathe normally and speak at a pace you can manage.

If you are waiting in a lobby or outside an office, choose a fixed point in the room and let your eyes rest there for a few seconds. Notice the chair under you or the floor beneath your shoes. That gives your attention something concrete to do instead of checking your internal alarm system for updates.

## Try a short breathing and grounding reset

Use this two-minute sequence just before the interview:

1. Breathe in normally through your nose if that feels comfortable.
2. Let the exhale run a little longer than the inhale, without forcing it.
3. Repeat for five breaths while keeping your shoulders loose.
4. Name five ordinary things you can see around you.
5. Feel both feet on the floor and notice one sound in the room.

Longer exhales can give you a steadier rhythm when your breathing has become quick and shallow. Keep the breaths quiet. Big, dramatic breaths can make you focus more on feeling anxious.

Grounding is useful here because it moves attention from predictions to what is actually present: a chair, a window, a sound, your feet on the floor. You are still nervous, perhaps, but you are no longer trying to solve the entire interview before it starts.

## Choose one useful intention

Interview anxiety often adds an impossible assignment: be impressive, confident, quick, likable, and flawless for the next thirty minutes.

Replace that assignment with one intention you can act on. For example:

- "I will answer honestly and use specific examples."
- "I will pause before I answer if I need a moment to think."
- "I will learn whether this role is a good fit for me, too."
- "I will be present for one question at a time."

An intention is not a promise about the outcome. It is a reminder of how you want to show up. You cannot control whether you get an offer, whether the interviewer likes every answer, or whether there is an unexpected question. You can control whether you listen, ask for a moment, and speak as clearly as you can.

That is a more realistic job for your attention.

## Arrive early enough to reduce avoidable stress

A calm before an interview usually starts with logistics. Give yourself time for the things that can make the last ten minutes harder: parking, finding the correct entrance, checking in, testing headphones, or opening the video link.

For an in-person interview, aim to be nearby early rather than walking through the door in a rush. For a video interview, test your camera, audio, charger, and link before it is time to join. Then step away from the screen for a minute instead of staring at the waiting room.

You do not need to use every spare minute to prepare. A little quiet time can keep a small delay or technical problem from becoming the whole story in your head.

## If your mind goes blank during the interview

A blank moment can feel much bigger than it looks from the other side of the table. You are allowed to pause.

Try a plain sentence such as, "Let me take a second to think about that," or, "I want to give you a useful example." Then take one normal breath and choose the closest relevant experience you have.

Most interviewers are not expecting a rehearsed speech. They are trying to understand how you think and work. A brief pause is usually less noticeable than rushing into an answer that does not say what you mean.

The same principle applies before a presentation. [Meditation before a presentation](/blog/meditation-before-a-presentation) can help when physical nerves are making it hard to begin, especially if you need to speak in front of a group.

## A reset to remember before you walk in

When anxiety before a job interview starts building, keep the reset practical:

1. Stop trying to rehearse every possible answer.
2. Loosen your jaw, shoulders, and hands.
3. Take a few quiet breaths with slightly longer exhales.
4. Ground yourself in what you can see, feel, and hear.
5. Choose one useful intention for the conversation.

You do not need to feel completely calm to interview well. You need enough space to use what you know, listen to the question, and answer from the real moment.
